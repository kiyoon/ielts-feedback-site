## Prompt

Governments give a lot of support to artists, even though some people think it is a waste of money that could have been used elsewhere. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

## Model answer (Band 7)

Whether governments should use tax revenue to support artists is contested. Advocates regard public funding as an investment in cultural life, whereas critics see extensive subsidies as a poor use of limited resources. Although the arts provide social value, I believe digital public services should receive greater priority because they can reach people more widely and efficiently.

The strongest case for subsidy is that some culturally valuable but commercially risky projects may be difficult to sustain without support. Popular productions can survive through ticket sales, but local theatre, traditional crafts, and free exhibitions may not generate enough revenue to cover their costs. A modest grant could help a community arts centre stage affordable performances or offer workshops to residents who might otherwise be excluded. By preserving these opportunities, public funding can protect cultural diversity while giving people constructive ways to relax and connect with others.

Nevertheless, public budgets are finite, so arts programmes must compete with digital services that address stress and loneliness more directly. A local authority could, for example, invest in moderated peer-support platforms, multilingual information portals, or accessible booking systems that connect residents with existing support. Unlike a grant benefiting one project and its audience, such infrastructure can be made available across an entire area through a shared platform. This does not mean that technology can replace artists; online exhibitions and creative platforms can instead distribute their work to wider audiences. In my view, these broader practical benefits make digital public services the stronger funding priority.

In conclusion, targeted grants can preserve worthwhile cultural projects that markets overlook and make artistic experiences more accessible. Even so, because well-designed digital services may support more citizens directly while expanding access to art, governments should avoid extensive general subsidies for artists and prioritise digital public infrastructure.

## Why this is Band 7 (and how it fixes the original)

- **Both policy cases are developed fairly** — the arts paragraph now explains
  which projects markets may not sustain and how a modest grant widens access;
  the digital-services paragraph compares public reach rather than simply
  asserting that technology is more efficient.
- **The writer's own position is preserved and qualified** — digital investment
  still receives priority, but the unsupported claim that technology can
  replace artists is explicitly rejected. Online platforms instead complement
  artists by distributing their work.
- **Public-finance collocations are natural** — *tax revenue*, *public funding*,
  *a modest grant*, *public budgets* and *general subsidies* replace recurring
  calques such as “consuming tax” and “spend the tax.”
- **Countability and articles are controlled** — *support* remains uncountable,
  while the countable policy noun appears correctly as *a modest grant*. Generic
  plurals such as *artists*, *projects* and *digital services* need no article.
- **Subjects survive complex sentences** — every finite verb has an explicit
  subject, avoiding “people who believes” and the bare-verb conclusion
  “provide them … can be regarded.”
- **Reference and synthesis are precise** — *such infrastructure* summarises the
  listed services, *these opportunities* refers to the arts provision, and the
  conclusion weighs cultural access against public reach in fresh language.
- **Controlled Band-7 range** — the answer demonstrates a *By + -ing* opener,
  defining and reduced relative clauses, concession, and a semicolon without
  sacrificing clarity.
